Many people feel nervous before a first appointment. It is hard to know what to say, or whether what you are struggling with is "serious enough". Knowing roughly what happens can make it easier.

## You do not need to prepare

You do not need a clear account of your difficulties, a diagnosis, or a goal. Practitioners are used to people arriving uncertain, and part of the first conversation is working out together what is going on.

If it helps, you could jot down a few notes beforehand: what made you seek support now, and anything you particularly want to ask.

## What you will probably be asked

Questions vary between practitioners, but often include:

- what has brought you here, and why now
- how things are affecting your sleep, work, relationships and daily life
- whether you have had support before, and what was or was not helpful
- what you would like to be different

You may also be asked about your safety, including whether you have had thoughts of harming yourself. This is a routine question asked of everyone. It is not a sign that the practitioner has made assumptions about you.

## You can choose what to share

You are in control of how much you say. It is fine to say "I'm not ready to talk about that yet". A good practitioner will respect that.

## What you should come away with

By the end, you should have a clearer sense of whether this kind of support suits you, how it would work in practice, and what it would cost. If you are offered ongoing sessions, it is fine to take time to think before deciding.

## Your questions matter too

A first conversation is also your chance to decide whether this practitioner is right for you. Our article on [choosing a therapist](/resources/choosing-a-therapist/) suggests questions to ask.
